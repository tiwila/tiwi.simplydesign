## The Problem

Midnight Run Cafe is a local staple in Waterloo — a study café, social hub, and community events space beloved by students and residents alike. The problem wasn’t the café itself. The problem was that their website told none of that story.

This was a 3-month academic UX research project with a team of four. I led the research: defining the research plan, coordinating methods across the team, and synthesizing findings into actionable design direction. Our next step is a full redesign proposal to pitch directly to the café.

The research problem was specific: the café had strong community presence and a loyal following, but its website failed at the most basic task — helping someone decide whether to visit. Users couldn’t find the menu, find hours, tell if it was a good study spot, or even confirm the café was still open. The homepage was essentially a single image and a newsletter signup.

Our research question: what specific usability and information gaps are preventing the Midnight Run Cafe website from supporting user decision-making — and what design changes would address them?

## The Why Moment

The turning point came when we stopped treating this like a visual redesign problem. The strongest signal across methods was trust: blank states, mislabeled navigation (“Products” instead of “Menu”), and irrelevant search results weren’t just confusing — they made the business feel less reliable.

Once we framed the goal as “reduce uncertainty in under 30 seconds,” the recommendations became obvious, measurable, and hard to argue with.

## What I Did

### Research plan (multi-method triangulation)
We designed a multi-method research approach to triangulate findings across expert evaluation, competitive context, and real user behavior.

- Heuristic evaluation (Nielsen’s 10) — identify usability and accessibility violations early, then form testable hypotheses for user sessions.
- Competitive analysis (Abe Erb Brewing + Eclipse Cafe) — establish a realistic benchmark for what local venue websites do well (menu clarity, events discoverability, navigation).
- User interviews — understand why people choose a café before they arrive (space, vibe, work suitability), not just what’s broken on the site.
- Task-based usability testing (4 participants, 7 tasks) — observe and quantify failure on real visit-decisions (menu, hours, study suitability, search).

### Heuristic evaluation (predicting the failures we’d measure)
We evaluated the site against Nielsen’s 10 heuristics before involving any users. Three violations stood out as most severe:

- Visibility of system status — the Products page displayed “0 products” with no explanation (no “coming soon,” no error state, no guidance).
- Match between system and the real world — searching “food” or “coffee” returned either nothing or irrelevant products; the system behaved like e-commerce, not a café.
- Recognition rather than recall — essential information (hours, menu, location, study suitability signals) was absent or buried, forcing users to guess where to click.

These heuristic violations predicted exactly what we later observed in usability sessions: uncertainty compounding into abandonment.

### Evidence-backed recommendations (making the redesign feel inevitable)
I synthesized the research into three findings, then translated them into three recommendations so the logic was traceable end-to-end.

Findings (the bridge):
- Critical information was absent at the decision point — users visit café sites to confirm (hours, menu, atmosphere), not to browse.
- Navigation didn’t match mental models — “Products” created confusion and reduced confidence before tasks even began.
- Missing feedback read as missing care — blank pages and irrelevant search results were interpreted as business unreliability, not “just a website issue.”

Recommendations:
- Improve content clarity — rename “Products” → “Menu,” surface hours/menu/vibe above the fold, and add explicit study/work suitability signals (Wi‑Fi, outlets, noise, seating).
- Improve navigation & structure — simplify labels, reduce hunting, and establish a clear hierarchy (Home → Menu / Hours / Events / About).
- Improve feedback & confidence — meaningful empty states (“Menu coming soon”), zero-result handling, and helpful search suggestions.

We used the usability results (5 out of 7 tasks at 0% success) to justify what to fix first.

## What Broke

More diverse participants. All four usability testers and most interview participants were university students. Midnight Run serves a broader Waterloo community — professionals, families, regulars. With more time, I’d run a second round with non-student users to add important texture to the findings.

Earlier competitive benchmarking. We ran heuristic evaluation before competitive analysis. In hindsight, doing them in parallel would have flagged the navigation label problem (“Products” vs “Menu”) earlier — and let us test it more directly during usability sessions.

## The Outcome

Research complete · redesign in progress · proposal pending. We produced a clear, defensible case for change: where the site breaks decision-making, which failures matter most, and what to fix first — grounded in multi-method evidence rather than preference.

What this project taught me as a research lead: research findings are only as useful as the story you tell about them. The data was clear early, but the harder skill was presenting it so the recommendations felt inevitable, not just recommended.

What’s next: we’re designing the redesigned website with the intent to propose it directly to Midnight Run Cafe. If they accept, this becomes a live implementation case study — and this page will be updated.
